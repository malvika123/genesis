# A minimal body layer for hominins: what the evidence supports (18 Nov 2026) — a draft; not built

**Why.** The owner (18 Nov): "can we build bones and muscle in hominins?" — skills such as swimming and tool use should come from a body and a brain, not from a number we raise by a fixed rule. A full body model (skeleton, muscles, mechanics) is a long-term goal; this note holds the smallest part with evidence behind it.
Evidence: `docs/reviews/2026-11-18-body-sources.md` (a research agent; abstracts and snippets only; many numbers unverified).

**Today.** A body is a few numbers: mass, speed, health, energy, age; each act has a fixed effect.

**Supported enough to use (with the uncertainty carried):**
1. **Body mass by sex:** *A. afarensis* about 45 kg (male) and 29 kg (female) (McHenry 1992, via Brassey et al.): medium. Lucy's own estimates 20–30 kg (Porter 1995 about 25 kg; Brassey et al. 2018 20.4 kg, 95% 13.5–30.9). Dimorphism contested (Reno & Lovejoy 2015: moderate, like modern humans; Gordon et al. 2008: larger, nearer gorillas): use the ratio about 1.5 only as an upper case, a registered assumption.
2. **Cost of walking:** about 6 J/kg/m at about 1 m/s, speed up to about 1.3 m/s (Sellers et al. 2005, a simulation: medium); below quadrupedal apes, above modern humans (Pontzer, Raichlen & Sockol 2009). Climbing: Stern & Susman 1983 (significant tree-climbing adaptations) against Ward 2002 (inconclusive): **a split view, one tunable hypothesis, not a measurement**.
3. **Muscle:** chimpanzee muscle about 1.35–1.5 times the force and power of human muscle per unit of muscle (O'Neill et al. 2017, *PNAS* 114:7343: 1.35 is a model, 1.5 a literature average): high for the range; whether australopiths sat nearer chimpanzees or humans is unverified: a single registered setting.
4. **Throwing:** the shoulder and arm changes for fast throwing first appear together in *Homo erectus* about 2 Ma (Roach et al. 2013, *Nature* 498:483): none for australopiths at 2.5 Ma: hand-held striking and short lobbing at most.
5. **Fractures (optional later, low confidence):** about 20% of wild apes show a healed fracture (Schultz 1956; Lovell; Jurmain 1989 and 1997), more in arboreal species and old animals; adult long-bone breaks are often fatal (Bulstrode et al. 1986 argue museum samples overstate healing); no healing-time number found.

**Do not model yet:** Lucy's death (Kappelman et al. 2016 fall from a tree, disputed); limb lengths or the intermembral index (no verified numbers); a muscle-mass percentage; arm strength for australopiths; bone density or growth; swimming and buoyancy (no source); throwing power.

**Smallest build, in order (each behind a switch, prediction first, identical worlds off, both ends of any uncertain number):**
1. Body mass by sex and age (replacing the generic mass), range 20–30 kg for females.
2. One climbing-against-walking trade-off: walking cost about 6 J/kg/m, a speed cap near 1.3 m/s, a climbing factor labelled as a hypothesis; replaces the single speed number; gives a reason to travel on the ground and sleep in trees.
3. Relative muscle strength: one tunable setting (1.35–1.5 × modern human).
4. Learned swim stroke (a brain action with a physical effect and no skill number; Opus reviews the design first), and a fracture risk, later.

**Open for the owner.** Whether to start with item 1 and 2 after the several bands, or after the brain probes; whether the swim stroke comes before the body layer.
