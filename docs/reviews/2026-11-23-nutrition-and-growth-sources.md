# Does growth respond to food? Evidence for apes and people (23 Nov 2026) — research agent, read only; search summaries, most full texts not read

**One line:** yes, growth can respond to food, but only the direction and rough size are backed by evidence; a growth-per-percent-deficit dose-response was NOT found, so the supply-factor rule must be a registered assumption tested at both ends.

| Finding | Value | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wild Gombe chimpanzee adult mass | median 39 kg male, 31.3 kg female; female growth slows about age 10, male about 13 (a later paper says females gain to about 21: unreconciled) | Pusey et al. 2005, *Int J Primatol* 26:3 | high (mass); medium (timing) |
| Food and mass in wild chimpanzees | heaviest during banana provisioning; heavier in the wet season, tracking rain in the prior month; mass swings larger in young and old than in prime adults | same | high (direction); no kg size found |
| Captive against wild | zoo and research adults heavier than wild-born sanctuary chimpanzees; zoo males reached adult mass at 12.2 y against 13.8 y in a sanctuary; zoo biscuit denser in calories; no kg or % found | Curry et al. 2022, *Zoo Biology* (PMC10084351) | medium |
| Fast breeding costs growth | juvenile size fell with shorter birth intervals (early weaning), not with maternal depletion | Emery Thompson et al. 2016, *PNAS* 113:7780 | medium-high |
| Energy balance gates breeding | mothers' C-peptide low about 6 months after birth, then rises; cycling returns only after sustained energy gain | Emery Thompson et al. 2012, *Behav Ecol* | medium |
| C-peptide tracks fruit | male C-peptide followed fruit in the diet; Ngogo (richer) higher than Kanyawara | Emery Thompson et al. 2009, *Horm Behav* | medium |
| Height heritability | 0.69–0.84 men, 0.53–0.78 women, about 0.80 typical; no evidence it is lower in poor populations | Jelenkovic et al. 2016, *eLife* 5:e20320 | high |
| Height trend with diet | up to about 20 cm (South Korean women) and 16.5 cm (Iranian men) over birth years 1896–1996; little in parts of Africa and South Asia | NCD-RisC 2016, *eLife* | high |
| Human catch-up | strong catch-up after 24 months and again in adolescence, even without intervention | Prentice et al. 2013, *Am J Clin Nutr* (PMC3628381) | medium-high |
| Tsimane stunted children | gained about 1 height z-score by age 11, about 0.11 SD/year faster than peers, yet most still short | Blackwell et al. 2017, *AJPA*; Brandeis papers | medium |
| Immune load against growth | up to 49% growth cut in periods of raised immune activity (population not confirmed) | Urlacher and colleagues | low-medium |
| Protein against energy trial | adding protein gave no extra growth over energy alone; energy intake best predicted height and weight gain; benefit only under age 3 | INCAP Guatemala trial 1969–77 and follow-ups | medium |
| Child protein need | maintenance 0.66 g/kg/day plus growth; totals about 1.14 (1 y), 0.97 (2 y), 0.87 (4–6 y), 0.92 (7–10 y), about 0.9 (11–14 y) | FAO/WHO/UNU 2007 | medium |
| Brain against body growth | brain uses about 66% of resting body energy at age 4; a trade-off with body growth argued, critiqued as small | Kuzawa et al. 2014, *PNAS* | medium |
| Ape energy | humans burn about 400–820 kcal/day more than great apes after size control | Pontzer et al. 2016, *Nature* | high (not growth cost) |

**Recommendation (the agent's).** Each step: growth = normal-curve growth × a supply factor = the lower of energy supply/need and protein supply/need, capped at 1; a floor (for example 0.2: a hungry child slows hard but never reverses: no source for the number); catch-up after a lean spell up to about 1.2–1.5 × normal until back on the curve (assumption; the Tsimane and Gambia evidence says catch-up is real but partial); genetic ceiling: adult size = inherited potential (heritability about 0.8) times how much of growth was delivered, early-life shortfall a partial permanent loss; **energy first** (INCAP: energy limits growth more than protein; protein matters mainly when very low: a hypothesis); seasonal swings dent mass (wild chimpanzees lighter in the dry season; breeding speed and juvenile size traded off). **Supported:** direction; heritability range; catch-up exists; energy matters more than protein. **Registered assumptions, tested at both ends:** the slope, the floor, the catch-up cap, the permanent-loss share, the protein threshold; test always fed (should approach captive-like size, a little heavier than Gombe) and chronically 70% fed (clearly stunted but alive and breeding later). **Refuted by:** well-fed wild-born apes not growing faster than Gombe; a population on half rations still reaching full adult size; protein-only shortage stunting as hard as energy shortage.
**Could not verify:** any dose-response (growth rate per % deficit); cm of adult height lost to chronic child undernutrition; Kibale or Gombe weight loss over a lean season in kg; Taï and Hamada growth curves; Rothman; age at first birth against food; Kuzawa 1998 itself and Pontzer 2012; share of energy spent on growth in apes; Hadza growth; menarche delay; kwashiorkor against marasmus; secular trend in apes.
Sources: Pusey 2005 · Emery Thompson 2016 (PMC4948337), 2012, 2009 · Curry 2022 (PMC10084351) · Prentice 2013 (PMC3628381) · Jelenkovic 2016 · NCD-RisC 2016 (PMC4961475) · Blackwell 2017 · INCAP summaries (archive.unu.edu) · FAO/WHO/UNU 2007 · Kuzawa 2014 (PMC4246319) · Pontzer 2016.
