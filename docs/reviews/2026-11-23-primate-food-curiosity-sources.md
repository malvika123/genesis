# How apes, monkeys and children come to try new foods (23 Nov 2026) — research agent, read only; search summaries and abstracts only (PubMed and PMC full texts blocked)

**Short answer:** young apes and monkeys sample new foods more than adults, mostly by copying their mother; no source found measures a hunger-free "taste curiosity" in numbers: the direction is well supported, the strengths are not.

| Finding | Value | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young orangutans copy the mother's food choices; diets closely match hers | mostly follow the mother; no teaching seen | Jaeggi et al. 2010, *Am J Primatol* 72:62 | high on direction, no numbers seen |
| Orangutan diet list adult-like by weaning | females 100% of the mother's list size by weaning, males about 80% (weaning about 6–8 years: my recall) | Schuppli et al. 2021, *Behav Ecol Sociobiol*; Schuppli et al. 2016, *Front Zool* | medium-high (second-hand) |
| Wild chimpanzee infants: eat alone in year 1 but only soft foods; diet breadth matches the mother's at about 4; stay close while she eats; play with food in step with her | ages 1–4 | Budongo (Estienne et al. 2019, *AJPA*) | medium |
| Young chimpanzees peer mostly at their mother; about 60% of socially learned behaviours concern food | 60% | Budongo peering study (authors and year unconfirmed) | medium-low |
| Wild capuchins: youngsters touch and eat novel foods more than adults; adults most cautious; all eat very little at first | qualitative | Visalberghi, Janson & Agostini 2003, *Int J Primatol* 24 | medium |
| Captive capuchin infants (4.5–12 months, 11 animals) show little caution toward new foods | 11 infants | Fragaszy, Visalberghi & Galloway (about 1997) via Addessi et al. 2009 (PMC2716038) | medium |
| Group-mates eating makes capuchins accept new food: general encouragement to eat, not food-specific learning | qualitative | Visalberghi & Addessi 2001 | medium |
| Age effect not universal: some primate studies find no age link with neophobia | mixed | Ventricelli et al. 2022, *Front Ecol Evol* | medium |
| Captive chimpanzees vary hugely, from almost fully neophobic to accepting nearly everything | large individual spread | captive chimpanzee novel-food study (istc.cnr.it/it/node/71044) | medium |
| Children's food neophobia peaks around 2, typically through 4–6 | 2–6 years | ACAMH summary; Wardle group | medium-high |
| Children may need 8–15 tastes to accept a new food; daily offers for 2 weeks raised intake by 64% | 8–15; +64% | review citing Birch & Marlin 1982, Birch et al. 1987, Wardle et al. | medium (8–15 second-hand) |
| Lactase persistence arose after dairying: about 7,500 years ago in central Europe, selected within the last 10,000 years | 7,500 / 10,000 | Itan et al. 2009, *PLoS Comput Biol* 5:e1000491 | high (Europe only) |

The "milk and cheese" point holds: the habit came first and the gene followed (a human-only parallel).
**Recommendation (the agent's):** one general "taste curiosity" pull not tied to any food. **Age:** strongest in infants and juveniles, falling to a low adult level by about 4–8 years (chimpanzees about 4, orangutans by weaning); adults keep a little; the shape comes from the sources, the exact curve is a guess. **Curiosity trait:** scale by individual curiosity (captive chimpanzees show a very large spread). **Hunger:** keep the existing hunger pull but let curiosity work when sated; hunger lowering neophobia is not shown in any source found: keep that link weak. **Fear:** a separate caution (neophobia), rising with age, high in adults, fading with repeated safe tastes (wild capuchins eat little at first contact). **Others eating:** a big boost if the mother is eating it, smaller for group-mates (the best-supported factor); include a pull to stay near the mother while she eats. **Acceptance:** tiny first tastes, rising over repeated exposures (human children 8–15; ape number unknown). **Strength:** only a qualitative ranking is supported: juvenile with mother eating > juvenile alone > adult with others > adult alone; no share of feeding time on novel foods was sourced: do not invent one. **Unsupported:** any food-specific innate liking; teaching by mothers (none seen in orangutans); a hunger boost to sampling. **Refuted by:** young animals sampling no more than adults; mother eating having no effect on what the young eat; or sampling that appears only when hungry.
**Could not verify:** "Gunst 2008" on orangutans (search found only a capuchin paper: use Schuppli 2016 and 2021); numbers from Jaeggi 2010; any share of feeding time on novel foods; any primate study of hunger and novel-food sampling; chimpanzee mouthing frequency in infants; Taï-specific results; the original Birch papers; whether any non-human primate tastes new things when not hungry for pure curiosity (the juvenile data suggest it; no direct test found).
