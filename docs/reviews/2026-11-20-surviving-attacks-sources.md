# How many predator attacks on primates are survived, and what saves them? (20 Nov 2026) — research agent, read only; Boesch 1991 and Isbell 1994 read in full text, the rest abstracts and snippets

**Best-supported figure: wild primates survive about 80–90% of leopard attacks (Taï estimate 82–91%); the most pessimistic observed count is about 55–65%. The sim's survival of about 30% is far below any figure found.**

**What saves them (strength of evidence).** Rescue and mobbing: supported (Taï: 11 alarm calls rushed, 9 leopards chased off; mothers and males reached a victim before the leopard; two named animals saved; Boesch notes this did not stop heavy losses): single anecdotes, not rates. Climbing and tree sleeping: accepted as a cause, no effect size (Isbell 1994; Anderson 1998; Bidner 2018). Alarm calls: vervets climb and give predator-specific calls; no count of escapes. Avoiding places: supported (vervet, samango); no numbers. Weapons: Boesch says only weapon use might work against leopards and chimpanzees used none; Treves & Palmqvist call sticks and stones "wholly inadequate": do not model weapons.
**Recommendation (the agent's).** (1) Make an attack non-lethal by default: a kill chance of about 10–20% per attack for a group of ape-like hominins; 40–45% is a defensible upper bound for attacks that draw blood (Taï 4 of 9); Busse's 36% is a lower-confidence, higher-risk case. (2) Add injury as a state (Taï: 6 of 9 victims wounded, 5 survived), with a later death from infection possible. (3) Rescue by group members: 11 rescues against 9 attacks at Taï; no sourced effect size: a free parameter; refuted if in-sim kill rates fall by less than a few percentage points with a nearby adult. (4) Most survival should come from not being attacked at all: Taï's attack rate 0.30–0.60 a year, death rate 0.055; keep the annual predator death rate near 3% (range 0–15%). (5) Tree sleeping reduces risk but sets no number (leopards succeed more at sleeping sites): reduce, do not remove night risk. (6) Do not build: stone throwing, fire, a group-size effect on individual risk, any hominin-specific kill rate (no source).
**Refuters:** annual predator deaths above about 6% per animal for ape-like hominins is more lethal than the worst known ape site; a kill share above 45% is above the observed bloody-attack ratio.
**Could not verify:** the Treves & Palmqvist numbers (the PDF host rejected the certificate); the Hill & Dunbar abstract; Seyfarth, Cheney & Marler 1980 escape counts; Kortlandt; Pruetz and Stewart nest heights; Gombe and Ngogo figures; the Busse 1980 full text; how many hominin skeletons show carnivore damage.
