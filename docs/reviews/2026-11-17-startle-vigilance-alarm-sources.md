# Startle, staying on guard, vigilance and alarm calls: what the evidence supports (17 Nov 2026)

Research agent, read only; the owner asked that these be added only with real evidence.

| Behaviour | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Acoustic startle: an inborn reflex that fades with repeats | **supported** (thunder in particular: weak, anecdotes only) |
| Staying guarded long after a scare | **no evidence** for behaviour: baboon looking returns to baseline **within 5 minutes**; stress hormones stay raised for days to weeks |
| Vigilance as a routine cost to feeding | **supported as a general pattern**, no clean numbers; many-eyes effect **mixed**; sentinels in primates **no evidence found** |
| Alarm calls: inborn sound, learned use, listeners respond | **supported** |

**1. Startle.** Rhesus whole-body startle test (Winslow, Parr & Davis 2002, *Biol Psychiatry* 51:859–866, abstract only); startle grows with loudness and fades with repeats. Squirrel monkeys (Parker et al. 2011,
*Psychoneuroendocrinology* 36:547–556): fades with repeats, grows with loudness; their own alarm "yaps" gave a bigger first startle than noise but faded faster; both raised stress hormones. Human babies: the Moro reflex is present at
birth and fades by 3–6 months (textbook sources, no primary study checked; no verified newborn sound threshold). Adult startle tests use noise bursts of about 65–116 dB for 40 ms; "about 80–85 dB for the first blink" is the agent's
background knowledge, **not checked**. Thunder: chimpanzee "rain displays" (Goodall, second-hand); sanctuary chimpanzees fled thunder at first and habituated (anecdotes).

**2. Vigilance after a threat.** Chacma baboons, 65 animals (Allan et al. 2024, *Commun Biol* 7:980, full text): looking highest during an alarm or encounter; "in all cases the animals returned to baseline levels of looking
within 5 min" (number of events not stated). Hours after a leopard killed an adult male (Allan, Bailey & Hill 2021, *Ecol Evol* 11:15404–15416, full text): flight and orienting distances unchanged, except slightly quicker orienting
in the first trial about 10 minutes after the alarm. Engh et al. 2006 (*Proc R Soc B* 273:707–712): females who lost a relative had raised stress hormones for about 4 weeks and groomed more (the 4-week figure via Allan 2021's
summary, not checked in Engh's text). Vervets (Seyfarth, Cheney & Marler 1980, *Science* 210:801–803): recorded alarm calls alone bring the right escape for each predator; alertness afterwards not measured.

**3. Routine vigilance.** Baboons (Allan 2024): while feeding they look more often but in shorter spells; at rest longer (it costs nothing); ground tasks such as digging trade off with feeding; planned looking in high-risk places
poorly supported; more neighbours within 5 m, less looking. Chimpanzees (Kutsukake 2006 *Ethology* 112:581–591; 2007 *Int J Primatol* 28:907–918, abstracts): more vigilant on the ground than in trees; it rises with more group
members near (mostly watching each other); percentages not obtained. Review (Allan & Hill 2018, *Am J Phys Anthropol* 165(S65):4–22): group-size effects in primates inconsistent; no common definition. Vervets (Isbell & Young 1993):
larger groups scanned less (via a summary).

**4. Alarm calls.** Chimpanzees with a model python (Schel et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e76674, full text): soft huu in 20 of 21 trials about 8 s after seeing the snake; alarm huu in 9 of 21 at about 62 s; waa bark in 7 of 21 at about 53 s;
alarm huus and waa barks rose when friends arrived; callers looked between the snake and others and kept calling until the others were safe; listeners looked, approached upright and scanned. Crockford et al. 2012 (*Curr Biol*
22:142–146): more calling when others had not seen the snake. Crockford, Wittig & Zuberbühler 2017 (*Sci Adv* 3:e1701742): more warning after hearing a calm "rest hoo" than an alert call. Bonobos give alarm barks (de Waal 1988,
second-hand). Inborn sound, learned use: macaques raised by another species changed their calls only a little (Owren et al. 1993, *Dev Psychobiol* 26:389–406); young vervets first give alarm calls to harmless animals and become
competent over about 4 years, partly by looking at adults (Seyfarth & Cheney 1986, *Anim Behav* 34:1640–1658, second-hand).

**5. What to model (each a hypothesis).** (a) **Startle to any sudden loud sound: yes, as a reflex of a few seconds** (flinch, stop, turn), not lasting fear; size grows with loudness from about 80–85 dB (unchecked); fades with
repeats, roughly halving within 5–10 harmless repeats; refuted by no fading or startle at ordinary levels. (b) **Staying guarded longer: do NOT add**; behaviour returns to normal within about 5–15 minutes; the sim's fear
halving every 1.5 hours is already longer than the evidence — test a much shorter half-life for scanning (about 2–5 minutes); a slow stress lasting weeks is justified only for hormones and grooming; refuted by field data of raised
scanning beyond 30 minutes. (c) **Vigilance as a cost to feeding: yes, as a general pattern without exact numbers** — almost free at rest, costs intake during ground foraging, more on the ground than in trees, fewer close
neighbours more looking; **not** a strong many-eyes effect, sentinels, or vigilance while sleeping. (d) **Alarm call: yes**, fixed sound, learning narrows what triggers it; an alert call within about 10 s; the louder alarm in under half
of encounters after about 50–60 s; more calling when others nearby have not seen the threat, stopping when they are safe; listeners look, approach and scan; refuted by calling that ignores who is near.

**Could not check:** dB levels in Winslow 2002; a newborn startle threshold; Kutsukake's percentages; full texts of Cowlishaw 1997 (*Anim Behav* 53:667–686) and Hill & Cowlishaw 2002 (*Eat or Be Eaten*, CUP, 187–204); the Engh
4-week figure in Engh's text; full texts of Seyfarth & Cheney 1986 and de Waal 1988; Bednekoff's sentinel review; an unattributed "persisting up to 30 min" snippet (not used).
