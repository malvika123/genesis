# Do apes swim, and how is it learned? (18 Nov 2026) — evidence for the sim's swimming rule

Research agent, read only; standard web search and one page fetch; primary full texts not read: "consistent", not "proven". The owner's question: "how do they just know swimming?"
**Do wild apes swim: rarely** (almost never; they wade in shallow water and avoid deep water).

**Apes.** Bender & Bender 2013, "First documented report of swimming and diving in apes", *Am J Phys Anthropol* 152:156–162, doi 10.1002/ajpa.22338 (via press coverage, phys.org; not the full text): a captive chimpanzee (Cooper) and a captive orangutan (Suryia), both raised by people; they do not dog-paddle but use a deliberate breaststroke-like frog kick;
the authors say humans and apes, unlike most mammals, "must learn to swim"; apes often drowned in deep zoo moats (press paraphrase); Cooper started diving minutes after safety ropes were strung over the deep end and surface swimming weeks later (ages not found; another summary says 5 individuals, not verified); the claim that apes' swimming is weak because of arboreal adaptation is the authors' hypothesis.
Wild chimpanzees: Fongoli chimpanzees soak in pools to cool off in the early rainy season (Pruetz & Bertolani 2009, as cited); no source on wild chimpanzees swimming. Bonobos: wade waist-deep to feed, not known to swim (Wisconsin primate fact sheet; bonobo-conservation page; Lola ya Bonobo: no captive bonobo reported to have learned). Gorillas wade at Mbeli Bai (secondary sources); no swimming found. Orangutans: rarely swim, avoid deep water, cross rivers by logs (conservation pages, anecdote).
**Drowning evidence beyond the Bender press summary: none; no counts.**

**Humans.** Newborns show crawl-like movements in water and a heart-rate drop on submersion (the diving response); they fade within weeks; holding the breath does not mean a baby can swim safely; reported AAP guidance: no evidence that infant swim programmes under age 1 reduce drowning (secondary, unverified): humans are **not born able to swim**. No numbers for learning time without lessons; nothing on Bajau children's ages (Ilardo et al. 2018, *Cell* 173:569: Bajau adults' spleens about 50% larger: a genetic adaptation in adults, not about learning).

**Early hominins.** Nothing found on 2.5 Ma hominins crossing deep water or swimming (Olduvai, Koobi Fora, Hadar not checked); from memory only: sea crossings by *Homo erectus* much later and the aquatic ape hypothesis unsupported: unverified.

**Recommendation (the agent's).** Supported: swimming is **learned, not innate**, in apes and humans; "Nobody taught them" is wrong in spirit; the best ape example had safety ropes and human-raised conditions. **Not supported:** ape-like hominins spontaneously becoming swimmers in about 42 hours of wading; wild apes appear to wade, not swim, even after a lifetime beside water; the one trained-ape case took weeks with a safety net.
Suggested change: deep water simply avoided with a high drowning risk, and **no learning that reaches "can swim" from wading alone**; keep wading (including upright wading); if the capacity is wanted at all: rare, slow (about 10 times slower) and gated (hand-reared or safe settings). Remove "Nobody taught them". Treat the 0.004 and 0.02 rates and "children twice as fast" as pure assumptions (no published learning rates).
**Refuted by:** a primary paper showing wild apes (or Pliocene hominins) routinely swimming across deep water; data showing unassisted children or other primates reaching competence within tens of hours; fossil or footprint evidence of deep-water crossing around 2.5 Ma.
