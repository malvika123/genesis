# How real primates and early hominins survived predators: group defence, sleeping sites, avoiding danger, many groups (17 Nov 2026)

Research agent, read only, at the owner's question (a band that grew to 82 was wiped out by 18 hunters by year 157). **Short answer:** real hominins most likely survived by being many bands spread over a wide area, not by
one band beating hunters; living primates fight back, which lowers losses but does not stop them; the sim's crash comes mostly from having one band and hunters that keep multiplying.

| Item | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Group defence / mobbing | **good** for chimpanzees (one strong field study); **weak** for bonobos, baboons; **absent** that leopards or hyenas avoid groups |
| Sleeping sites | **good** that apes and baboons choose high or cliff sites against predators; **weak** on how many deaths it prevents |
| Avoiding dangerous places | **good** that it happens (vervets, samangos); **absent**: effect sizes for baboons or apes |
| Early hominins and predators | **good** that some were eaten; **weak** for tree use; **absent** for defence with stones or sticks |
| Surviving as a population | **good** for predation about 3% a year; **absent**: any documented whole group wiped out by predators; **weak to good** for many groups plus dispersal (theory, few ape data) |
| Predator numbers | **good** for the carnivore-to-prey ratio; **weak** for predators increasing when one prey becomes common |

**1. Group defence.** Taï, 5 years, 29 leopard–chimpanzee interactions (Boesch 1991, *Behaviour* 117:220–242; summary read in the PDF): chimpanzees chased leopards away 9 times, rescued a chimpanzee giving alarm calls 11 times;
leopards attacked 9 times, wounding 6 and killing 4. About 0.30 attacks per chimpanzee a year, a 0.055 a year chance of being killed; leopards the leading cause of death; the community shrank by 16 in 5 years. Mothers and males
ran to help; males used sticks as clubs. Group defence did not stop leopards: 5 of 9 victims were in large parties; under high pressure parties were smaller but more often all-male. Kortlandt (1965): captive chimpanzees attacked a
stuffed leopard with sticks, screaming (details not checked). Bonobos: two males and a female harassed a leopard for 3 hours, nobody killed (Corredor-Ospina et al. 2021, *Primates* 62). Baboons: leopards the main killer of adults,
most successful at sleeping sites; adult males taken more often (Cowlishaw 1994, as cited); no checked numbers on male defence.

**2. Sleeping sites.** Fongoli chimpanzees (predators mostly removed) nest lower, farther apart, sometimes on the ground, compared with Assirik (predators present) (Pruetz et al. 2008, *Am J Primatol* 70:393–401); Fongoli mean about
8.3 m (secondary). Issa (many predators): higher and further out on branches than Fongoli, no forest preference, no better escape routes (Stewart & Pruetz 2013, *Am J Primatol* 75:593–604). Baboons strongly prefer cliffs; collared
leopards visited river sites more than cliffs; most baboon deaths still at or near sleeping sites (Bidner et al. 2018; Hamilton 1982); groups may abandon a site after a kill (Anderson 1998).

**3. Avoiding danger.** Vervets used areas less where leopards and baboons made them alarm-call more; samangos avoided areas with more eagle risk (Willems & Hill 2009, *Ecology* 90; Coleman & Hill 2014, *Anim Behav* 88:165–173);
some predators avoided, not others; effect sizes not obtained.

**4. Early hominins.** SK 54 (*Paranthropus*, about 1.5 Ma): two punctures 33 mm apart matching a leopard's lower canines (Brain 1970, *Nature* 225:1112–1119). Taung child probably killed by an eagle (Berger & Clarke 1995,
*J Hum Evol* 29:275–299; disputed on lifting weight). Lucy may have died falling from a tall tree (Kappelman et al. 2016, *Nature* 537:503–507; disputed). Hart & Sussman 2005, *Man the Hunted* (an argument, not data). Sticks,
thorn branches and stones "wholly inadequate" against a surprise attack by a large carnivore (Treves & Palmqvist 2007). No evidence of defence with weapons at 2.5 Ma.

**5. Persistence.** Median predation about 3% a year across 24 primate populations, 0 to over 15% (Cheney & Wrangham 1987, via Isbell 1994, *Evol Anthropol* 3:61–71); apes low. Rates can spike: Amboseli vervets lost at
least 45% in 1987 (14 in one 30-day period), then returned to normal (Isbell 1990, 1994). Shrunken vervet groups merged: 6 mergers 1984–88, the smallest group 3–6 animals with at least 2 adults (Isbell, Cheney & Seyfarth 1991;
Young & Isbell 1994). No documented case of predators wiping out a whole primate group. Persistence through patches, local extinction and recolonisation shown in howlers and lemurs (Gould, McLennan & Donati 2020), not chimpanzees or baboons.

**6. Predator numbers.** About 90 kg of a given carnivore per 10,000 kg of prey, about 1:111 (Carbone & Gittleman 2002, *Science* 295:2273–2276). Predators increase with total prey (snow leopards). Primates a small part of big cats'
diets: leopards about 10%, lions under 1% (Isbell 1994). A hunter population tripling on one band of 82 has no support. Prey switching: weak (non-peer-reviewed source).

**7. Recommendation (each a switch and a close hypothesis).** Most important: **more than one band** (with N ≥ 8 bands and young adults dispersing, the population lasts over 1,000 years at 3–5% predation; refuted if it still dies out
with predation held at 0–15% a year). **Cap hunter numbers by total prey mass (1:111)**, hominins a small part of their diet (refuted if predation on hominins stays above about 15% a year for years). Then: (a) **rally to a member's
alarm and chase the leopard** (Boesch: about one rescue or chase per attack, about 45% of attacks still wound or kill; refuted if mobbing drives leopard deaths to zero or mortality far below about 0.05 a year); (b) **sleep on high trees or
cliffs, and leave a site after a kill** (refuted if sleeping-site kills stop being the most common); (c) **small bands merge** below about 3–6 members or 2 adults. Second: avoid learned dangerous places (weak). **Do not model:**
weapons for defence; predators giving up because a group is large; prey switching.

**Not verified:** Kortlandt's details; the Cheney & Wrangham chapter text; full texts of Cowlishaw 1994, Willems & Hill, Coleman & Hill, Bidner; Pruetz 2008 nest numbers; baboon male defence data; Kortlandt 1980; Cheney et al. 2004's
"97% of deaths from predation".
