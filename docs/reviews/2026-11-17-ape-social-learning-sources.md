# How young apes learn by watching: sources for honest watching (17 Nov 2026)

Research agent, read only, for the design of honest watching (backlog: "How a hominin sees another's actions"). **Enough evidence to design honest watching: partly.** Good on how close, whom, what is watched and
that apes copy results more than actions; thin on how often and how long; almost nothing on wild bonobos. Several key papers were seen only as abstracts or summaries (marked).

## 1. Peering (close, attentive watching)
- Definition and distance: "attentive close-range watching… sustained over at least five seconds"; for nest-building close enough to see detail, 5 m (Permana et al. 2025, citing Schuppli 2016). A "peering range" of 0–2 m
  (Kukofka et al. 2025). Wild chimpanzees: a potential observer only within 1 m, not facing away, a clear view; a stricter group with the head within ±45° of the model (Hobaiter et al. 2014). Checked against the text.
- Whom: 83.9% of orangutan nest peering at the mother (Permana 2025); immatures peer mostly at older animals or peers, very rarely at younger ones (Kukofka 2025); zoo immatures peer more at non-mothers than wild ones;
  Bossou chimpanzees watched same-age or older individuals, not younger (Biro et al. 2003, abstract); young Budongo chimpanzees peered at experienced animals, often their mothers (Slania et al. 2026, press release only).
- What: wild orangutans 82% of peering during feeding, 14% nest-building, about 1% each exploring and social (Kukofka 2025); peering rises with how hard a food is to process and falls with how often the mother eats it
  (Schuppli 2016, secondary summaries); wild chimpanzees peered at 69 different actions, about 60% about food (Slania 2026, press release).
- Age: wild orangutan peering peaks at about 3–4 years (zoo 5–6) (Kukofka 2025). Gombe daughters watched mothers termite-fish more than sons (Lonsdorf 2004/2005, abstracts and news only).
- How often, how long: no rates per hour or bout lengths found in texts opened; Budongo 366 peering events in more than 1,000 hours, all ages (Slania 2026). Not verified.

## 2. What is copied
- Chimpanzees mostly copy results (emulation): with a see-through puzzle box they skipped useless steps; with an opaque box they copied the whole sequence (Horner & Whiten 2005). Chimpanzees "are much more focused on the
  outcome" and copy with low fidelity (Tennie, Call & Tomasello 2009). Zone of latent solutions: apes learn socially only what they could in principle invent; watching tells them where and what, not how (Tennie 2009).
- Copying does happen in the wild: each observation of moss-sponging raised a naive chimpanzee's learning rate about 15-fold (14.9×, 95% CI 4.7–88.2) (Hobaiter 2014).

## 3. What a watcher can see, from whom
- Watching needs line of sight and close range; being near without watching did not raise practice, peering did (Permana 2025). Sourced: 1–5 m.
- Mostly from the mother, not only: Taï immigrant females switched to their new group's hammer choice (Luncz, Wittig & Boesch 2015, summary only).
- Biases: captive chimpanzees copied an older, higher-ranking model with a record of successful innovations (Horner et al. 2010); they copy dominant and expert animals, and copy more when uncertain or of low rank (Kendal et al. 2015).
  Offspring of more efficient Taï mothers became more skilled; mothers' sharing of nuts had no clear effect (Estienne et al. 2019, abstract).

## 4. Calls
Ape call structure is "largely innate", with small group-level tuning; learning to understand calls is fast and open-ended; learning to produce new calls is not shown (Fischer & Hammerschmidt 2020). Watson et al. 2015 (zoo food grunts) is
disputed (Fischer, Wheeler & Higham 2015; reply Watson et al. 2015). Crockford et al. 2004 and Lameira not checked.

## 5. Time to master a skill (age by which most have basic skill; table in Musgrave et al. 2020)
| Skill | Site | Age (years) |
|---|---|---|
| Oil-palm nut cracking | Bossou | over 3.5 |
| Coula nut cracking | Taï | 3–4 |
| Panda nut cracking | Taï | 5 or more |
| Termite fishing | Gombe | 5.5 |
| Termite fishing | Goualougo | 2.9 |
| Perforating plus fishing | Goualougo | 10.5 |
Gombe females termite-fished successfully about 27 months earlier than males (Lonsdorf 2005, abstract). Bossou sensitive period 3–5 years (Biro 2003; secondary). Watching alone is not enough: Bossou infants at 2.5 years could not order the actions
(Inoue-Nakamura & Matsuzawa 1997); captive chimpanzees did not improve right after watching a skilled peer, practised the parts one at a time and first succeeded in sessions 8–15 of 30 minutes (Hirata et al. 2009, no control group).
Orangutan night nests take until about 8 years (Permana 2025). Bouts watched before mastery: not found.

## 6. Recommendation (S sourced, A assumption with a range)
- Watching only with line of sight, facing the elder, within about 1–5 m (S), for at least about 5 s (S); in the game 1–3 m (A).
- The youngster sees what the elder holds and where it is (S: stimulus or local enhancement), the result: food got and eaten, failure, a hurt (S: emulation), and a rough category of hand action (A). Not the elder's internal reward or its exact choice among 19 actions.
- Copy mainly results and targets (S); copy the action sequence only when the result is hidden or not understood (S, Horner & Whiten 2005).
- Whom: mother first (A: about 80% of peering; S for orangutans); extra weight to older, more skilled and higher-ranking animals; never younger ones (S).
- Peering strongest at 2–5 years and on rare or hard foods (S); watching raises the chance of practising the same item over the next hour (S, orangutans); practice still needed; mastery at 3–6 years (S); watched bouts per skill tens to hundreds (A).
- Calls: no learning of new calls; at most small tuning of existing ones (S).

**Could not verify:** peering rates and bout lengths for chimpanzees; Bossou infant distances; the Lonsdorf watching percentages and "31 vs 58 months"; the full Schuppli 2016 and Slania 2026 texts; bonobos.
