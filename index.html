    Every creature is a reinforcement-learning agent with its own personality. Its eyes report what lies in eight directions, its body reports hunger, thirst, tiredness and pain, and a neural network chooses what to do: walk, stay, eat, drink, sleep, mate, attack, or share food. Survival is wired in like a brainstem: when starving it reaches for food in front of it, when parched it drinks. Everything else is learned from what its body feels. It can only fall asleep when tired, then sleeps until rested or woken by pain. Children can’t mate: people reach that at 16 (hunter-gatherers have their first child around 19 to 20), predators at 4, herbivores at 1.5. A child gets a random mix of its parents’ wiring, and only habits a parent learned strongly can pass on, and only partly.

    Human minds differ from the animals in the ways research points to: a bigger brain, a long childhood and a learning window that stays open well into adulthood (animals stop learning as much once grown), a strong curiosity that rewards finding new places, imitation of elders, and a long memory of where food and water were. People also learn from what happens to others: seeing someone near them bitten, burned, fall or get sick, they feel a weaker share of it and a jolt of fear, family most, children most of all, and turn a little away from what they saw lead there. Each is born a different kind of learner: some hardly learn from others at all, some copy those doing well, some go with the crowd. People are born able to form pair bonds, not with a rule to be faithful: a bond grows with time together and fades apart, the partner is the one they are most bonded to, and whether they stay together is up to them. Fish live in the ponds and eat algae. People can learn to catch them, and predators hunt anything, including each other when starving.

    People are little figures with a pink outline (female) or blue (male) — no clothes, as the first humans had none; yellow-green ovals are herbivores, red diamonds are predators, white fish swim in the water, faded means asleep. Rivers and ponds have shallow edges for drinking and a darker deep middle that can drown you. Black holes are pits that trap and hurt. Red dots on grass are poisonous berries. One cell is about 3 km, one step is 15 minutes, and a year is squeezed into 3 days. Click a creature to see its thoughts.

    The world is a round planet. Drag it to spin it, scroll or pinch to zoom, or switch to the flat map with the button at the top. The sun circles the planet once a day, so it is day on one side and night on the other. Walk east far enough and you come back from the west. The top is near the north pole and the bottom near the south pole, so the middle is warm and the edges are cold. A year has four seasons, opposite in the two halves, like on Earth. In winter grass stops growing, ponds near the poles freeze over (you can walk on ice but not drink from it) and snow covers the ground. People have no fur and feel cold below about 14 °C; herbivores and predators have thick winter coats and only feel the cold below about -10 °C. Being cold burns energy and freezing hurts, but huddling next to your own kind helps. Nobody is told any of this: they have to learn to stay warm.

    The land is the real Earth as it was about 70,000 years ago, when our species was leaving Africa: today's continents, but with the sea about 80 m lower, so Asia and Alaska, Southeast Asia's islands, New Guinea and Australia, and Britain and Europe were joined by dry land. The first band starts where our species lived then: in Africa, in warm country of grass and woodland near fresh water. (Simplified: each square of the map is about 300 km of real Earth; there are no ice-age ice sheets yet, and the climate is today's.) A new world can instead be a made-up planet. Researchers can run many seeded worlds at once and compare two setups on the Lab page.

    Realistic, in the View menu, adds real-looking water (rippling, glinting, mirroring the sky at a low angle) and a real sky near the ground (blue overhead, red toward a low sun, stars at night). It is off unless you turn it on, because it is slower to draw, and it changes only the picture, not the world.

    Land and sea: the ocean is salty and undrinkable, shallow by the coasts and deep further out. Continents rise into mountains (on the real Earth each square holds the typical height of its 300 km, so Tibet stands about 5 km high rather than Everest's 8.8; on a made-up planet the very highest summits reach 8.8 km and the deepest sea floor 11 km), with caves in the stony hills for shelter. The dead stay where they fell and become bones, where it is much colder and snow stays on the peaks. Rain drains downhill into rivers and fills lakes in the basins. Climbing is tiring, and mountains wring the rain out of the wind, leaving dry land behind them.

    Animals are real kinds that lived alongside early humans. Plant-eaters: deer (graze and browse at the woodland edge), rabbits (small, breed fast, short lives) and bison (big, slow-breeding herds on open grassland, hard to bring down). Hunters: wolves (live and hunt in packs, handle hard cold), big cats (warm lands, hunt alone or in pairs) and bears (big, eat fruit and plants as well as meat, handle deep cold). Each kind only breeds with its own kind. Bigger prey is harder to bring down alone, so hunters of the same kind hunting together succeed more often, and a big kill feeds the whole pack. How much a plant-eater eats follows its body mass, as in real animals: a bison eats far more grass than a deer and a rabbit very little, but small bodies burn their reserves faster. Grazed grass grows back from its roots. Tap the counts at the top to find one.

    What people eat: like us, they cannot digest grass. They are born, like real babies, liking sweet (ripe fruit) and savoury, fatty tastes (meat, food roasted by a wildfire), and when hungry they try putting plants in their mouths. Everything else they must find out: that roots and tubers lie under certain plants and can be dug up (much easier with a stick), that a fresh body is food (a sharp stone cuts it far faster), that meat left a few days rots and makes you sick, and that a rabbit or a deer can be caught — though a bison or a deer can hurt you back. Hunters and bears scavenge bodies too; flies and vultures take what is left, faster in the heat, and where a body rots the grass grows lusher.

    Forests: trees grow where it is warm and wet enough — rainforest near the equator, woods in the middle latitudes, pines in the cold and up the mountains to a treeline. They bear fruit people can eat, give shelter from rain and wind, hide creatures from each other, and slow you down. Zoom in on the planet to see the trees.

    Vision: each creature sees only in front of it, over its kind's real field of view (about 200° for people, almost all the way round for deer), and hills and thick forest hide what is behind them. At night it sees as far as its night vision allows; asleep its eyes are shut. Only people tell red from green. Tap a creature and choose “Its eyes” to see from where it stands.

    Feelings: hunger, thirst, tiredness, pain, cold and heat, fear, anger after being attacked, missing those it is bonded to when they are long out of sight, distress on finding one of their bodies (no one is ever told of a death), loneliness, longing, care for its young, the fun of play, boredom, curiosity and contentment — the core emotional systems of all mammals. They change what it does and learns; whether it experiences them, no one can say.

    Talking: people can make six sounds while they walk or run. A baby’s cry and a scream of pain are inborn; the other four mean nothing until use gives them meaning. They hear each other, and answering pleases both. The Stats tab shows what each sound has come to mean, worked out only from when it is used. Tap a person and choose “Listen” to hear them. Children inherit genes for brain size, learning and copying — not what their parents learned, which passes on only by copying.

    Fire: it needs dry fuel — grass, trees, sticks — and something hot enough. In nature it comes from lightning (and, rarely, sparks from falling rocks), spreads with the wind and uphill, and goes out in the rain. Animals flee it; people are only wary. Fire cooks food, warms, lights the night and keeps predators away. For a long time early humans could only keep fire: take it from a wildfire, carry a burning stick, feed it, and lose it to the rain. Making fire, with sparks from pyrite (a rare shiny stone) or by working dry wood, is a skill: almost impossible at first, quick once practised, learned faster by those used to fire and picked up by watching. Nothing tells them any of this.

    Storms: thunderstorms build where the sun-heated ground lifts warm, moist air — on hot, humid afternoons. Lightning strikes from them and thunder startles creatures nearby.

    Hands: people can pick up the stones and fallen sticks that lie about, put them down, and strike with them. Nobody tells them what that is good for. The world just behaves like Earth: a stone hit against a nut breaks its hard shell, two stones struck together can knock off a sharp flake, and a stick reaches further than an arm. All they are born with is what human babies have — a grasping hand and a liking for banging things. Simplified: a hand holds one thing, and the nut is eaten as soon as it is cracked.

    Weather: water evaporates from lakes, rivers and wet ground, the wind carries it, and where air rises and cools (along the equator and in drifting storms) it condenses into clouds and falls as rain, or snow where it is freezing. The belts around 30° stay dry, like Earth’s deserts. Grass needs wet soil to grow, heavy rain leaves puddles creatures can drink from, and getting rained on makes you colder. Use “Hide clouds” to see the ground underneath.

    Whispering: click a creature, then type anything in the floating box that appears. It reaches them in their sleep or a quiet moment, as their own thought. Your words are read for the ideas in them (water, food, fish, berries, hunters, pits, sharing, others, sleep, a partner, exploring, a direction) and whether you say do or don’t. Each becomes a small nudge in their brain, never a command. Words that mean nothing in their world stay a vague feeling.
